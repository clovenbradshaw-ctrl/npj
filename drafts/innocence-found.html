Innocence Found

What we can learn from Nashville attorney Jason Gichner, Executive Director of Tennessee Innocence Project — and a round-up of recent developments in Tennessee's fight against wrongful conviction.
By Elizabeth MK Sullivan · Edited by Mike Lacy

Joyce Watkins could have thrown her boyfriend Charlie Dunn under the bus when they were both on trial for first-degree murder in Nashville in 1988 when the DA asked her to testify against Charlie as their trial was coming to a close, but she chose not to.

Instead, the jury convicted Joyce and she received a life sentence in prison for a crime she did not commit.

Some 27 years later, the nonprofit legal organization Tennessee Innocence Project (TIP) made right what was so deeply wrong.

Joyce was proven “actually innocent,” exonerated by the facts, as experts determined the central argument in the case had no basis in forensic science. Joyce was paroled in 2015, but not exonerated until 2022. During those years, Joyce was required to remain on the Tennessee sex offender registry, in the parole system and existing under significant restrictions.

This all cost Joyce a quarter century of her life path, but she would not back down from the truth. Joyce lost out on building a life during those years — spending time with her friends and family, continuing a career path, reaching milestones, and all the other things that might have been.

And if all of this wasn’t heartbreaking enough, the costs are so high.

The expenses associated with exonerating someone convicted of a life sentence are astronomical. And, last but not least, the cost of housing a person in prison for 27 years to Tennessee’s taxpayers is utterly wasteful.

This is a story where everybody loses.

And if there’s one thing that TIP Executive Director Jason Gichner would like us to know – what we miss – it’s that there really are actually innocent people in Tennessee prisons – and at an alarming rate.

“We don’t want to believe that we have a system in which this happens,” Gichner says. “We want to believe that when a jury says ‘Guilty’ that we can believe it.”

Thus, for those caught in this nightmare, justice becomes an illusion. A bedtime story for us to fall asleep to. But Goldilocks, this ain’t. And for those who choose to look the truth in the face and make good with reality, it becomes impossible to look away.

Currently more than 50,000 people are held in Tennessee jails as well as state and federal prisons. When compared to nations around the world, the incarceration rate in the United States is shockingly high, and the rate in Tennessee is even higher. That’s a topic for another day. The importance here is that if even a small percentage of Tennessee prisoners are wrongly convicted and incarcerated in just state prisons, for example, current estimates from TIP indicate that group alone totals more than 1,000 Tennesseans. It’s far easier to put an innocent person in prison than to get them out, Gichner explained.

With exceptional advances in forensic science over recent decades, it’s important to look back with sincere reflection on those incarcerated who stand by their innocence and who may have been convicted on faulty forensics and debunked investigative methods.

No doubt highly controversial and profitable private prison operator CoreCivic, headquartered in Brentwood, Tenn., which owns and/or operates private prisons in our state and across America – including ICE facilities – may be disappointed to hear this.

CoreCivic is paid by the taxpayers per prisoner. That helps CoreCivic executives to reportedly receive significant financial compensation for what should never be a matter of economics. Worth mentioning from just days ago – CoreCivic CEO Patrick Swindle stepped down unexpectedly due to a major healthcare issue and Lucibeth Mayberry was appointed new president and CEO September 25.

In the news in recent weeks – outside prosecutors have decided not to pursue charges against Nashvillian Calvin Atchison, who was charged with murder in 2021 of a Nashville woman killed in 2000 after an innocent man, Paul Shane Garrett, was wrongly convicted of the murder in 2004 and spent a decade in prison.

In the meantime, former Nashville District Attorney Torry Johnson, as well as a surprisingly long list of professionals in the Nashville law and order system (former Police Chief Steve Anderson, Public Information Officers Don Aaron for MNPD and Susan Niland for the DA’s office at the time, and later at TBI, and then-Assistant DA Tom Thurman) knew years ago that, very likely, the wrong person had been convicted, as detailed in numerous news reports and public court documents – including a court order.

“The extent of the intentional wrongdoing on so many occasions and at all levels of local law enforcement is so flagrant a violation of fundamental fairness that such conduct should be considered a due process violation standing alone…” wrote Davidson County Criminal Court Judge Mark Fishburn (Ret.) in an astonishing opinion he signed in December 2022 regarding Atchison and Garrett. “Their intentional decisions were the act of cowards to hide the truth for fear of public humiliation and legal retribution that might arise if their wrongdoing saw the light of day.”

To his credit, MNPD Detective Michael Roland tried to take action on the issue and was prevented from doing so, according to Judge Fishburn’s order. Garrett was exonerated by exceptional legal work conducted by TIP.

Even more recently, Governor Bill Lee commuted the life sentence of prisoner and TIP client Thomas Clardy last month. Clardy was wrongly accused and incarcerated for a murder at an auto body shop in Madison in 2005. He was released from prison last week.

And so, the honorable fight for justice continues here in Tennessee and across America.

And about Charlie, Joyce’s boyfriend, turns out he didn’t commit a murder or any such horrific crime either. He was also “actually” innocent. After 27 years as a captive of the state of Tennessee, Charlie Dunn died in prison in 2015. Seven years later, the charges against Charlie were officially dismissed.

Additional contextual statistics from TIP’s website about the organization’s innocence work:

  • Since its founding in 2019, TIP has helped secure 10 exonerations—accounting for a fourth of all known exonerations in Tennessee.
  • TIP’s 10 exonerations represent over 300 years of wrongful imprisonment.
  • TIP’s exonerees had more than 500 years total remaining on their sentences at the time of exoneration.
